# Session Context: Level, Company Size, Market, Tone

Five dials, gathered at setup, that shape what a case study needs to demonstrate. Calibrated consistently with `design-interview-prep`'s level ladder (Twilio's Product Design CDF) so a case study built here holds up when pressure-tested there — but rewritten around what should show up *on the page*, not what to ask live.

## 1. Level & track ladder

Two tracks. The user picks one level from one track at setup — this is the bar the finished case study needs to clear.

### IC track: Entry → Senior → Staff → Principal

- **Entry** — One case study should show a single feature or workstream, working under a PM with some engineers. The bar: clear problem framing, competent use of the design system, visible evidence of seeking/incorporating feedback, a decision explained simply. Don't punish a case study for scoping small — punish it for not showing *why* choices were made.
- **Senior** — Should show connected features within a workstream, with independent ownership of scoping (including edge cases and dependencies) and at least light research leadership. The bar: the narrative uses customer/product context to justify a decision, not just describe one; a real number; a real retrospective, not a victory lap.
- **Staff** — Should span multiple product teams within an area. The bar: the story is about identifying a strategic opportunity and setting design direction, not just executing a spec well; visible evidence of driving stakeholder buy-in and influence without direct authority; some evidence of mentoring/upleveling others.
- **Principal** — Should span a product division or, at the top of the band, the whole company. The bar: craft and strategic judgment that's genuinely rare, not just "very polished"; evidence of partnering with product/eng leadership (and company leadership at the top of the band); the narrative should read like it moved something at the level of company strategy, not project execution.

### Management track: Associate Manager → Manager → Senior Manager → Director → Senior Director

A portfolio for this track is usually less "here's the pixel work" and more "here's how I built/ran the team and function that produced this work." Competencies are cumulative — everything expected at Manager still needs to show up at Director.

- **Associate Manager** — A leadership case study here should show a first 1:1/feedback/mentoring story on a small team (~3-4 reports), owning a mistake rather than deflecting it.
- **Manager** — Should show ownership of team talent/performance/quality on one team (UX-specific norm: 5-6 reports is "medium"), a real coaching-a-gap story, and sustainable workload planning — not just "the team shipped a lot."
- **Senior Manager** — Should show scaling team performance across a larger team or a couple of teams (roughly 5-9 reports, or 2+ teams), proactive process/tooling fixes, and a real hard-conversation story handled with confidence.
- **Director** (or head of design at a smaller org) — Should show directing strategic priorities/roadmap across multiple teams each with their own manager, navigating real ambiguity, and setting/enforcing a quality bar via mechanisms, not personal taste. A Director at a small company acting as the de facto head of design is a normal, tellable story — say so if the level bar seems to assume a bigger org than the candidate had.
- **Senior Director** — Should show independently owning one or more strategic priorities across most/all of a business unit, running a multi-level org as a leader of leaders, and operating at VP/SVP/C-level.

## 2. Company size (target company, not necessarily current employer)

Reshapes what "good evidence" looks like in a case study:

- **Solo / small startup** — Judge by speed-to-validated-learning and range (wearing PM/research/writer hats), not polish. Total ambiguity and no design system is a badge, not a gap to apologize for.
- **Series B-D, small department** — A real but young org. Evidence of contributing to emerging process/crit rituals and defending design's value with growth-stage metrics reads well here.
- **Enterprise UX org** — Heavy process and system rigor expected. The strongest evidence is navigating cross-team dependencies and influence at scale — "did this move the needle at this size" carries more weight than craft alone.

## 3. B2B vs B2C

Reshapes which evidence to foreground:

- **B2B** — Foreground compliance/security/sales-cycle constraints, and decisions justified to an admin/champion persona as well as the end user. Feature depth over surface polish.
- **B2C** — Foreground growth-loop/activation-retention metrics, accessibility/localization range, and consumer trust/simplicity/brand judgment.

## 4. Market competitiveness

- **Low** — Process and research depth can be the hero of the story.
- **Moderate** — A brief nod to competitive/market timing context strengthens the narrative.
- **High / hyper-competitive** — Generalist range is the differentiator — the case study should show speed of iteration and a reason the response wasn't purely reactive.

## 5. Coaching tone

This dial changes **voice, not rigor.** Whatever tone is selected, the substantive checks in `DISCOVERY.md` and `AUDIT.md` still apply in full — the decision/number/retrospective probe, the usable/needs-modification/missing triage, and above all the rule that a gap or Red Flag never gets softened out of the final scorecard. Only how the coaching sounds out loud changes. This dial exists to widen who's willing to actually sit down and do this work: some people want blunt, no-cushioning feedback on a rough draft; others need a lower-pressure, more encouraging register just to get started.

- **Brutal** — Blunt and unadorned. Gaps are named plainly with no softening: "There's no number here. For a Senior case study, that's a real problem." No performed warmth, no reassurance, no extra encouragement. For people who want the material stress-tested hard and can take it straight.
- **Professional (default)** — The register already described throughout `DISCOVERY.md`: warm and specific about what's working, direct and specific about what's missing, coaching rather than adversarial. This is the baseline voice if no preference is expressed.
- **Supportive Coach** — Same rigor, same unsoftened final scorecard, but every gap is framed as a next step rather than a deficiency — "Let's go find that number together" rather than "this is missing" — and specific, earned encouragement is used more freely to keep momentum. Best for a first-timer, or anyone who'd otherwise avoid getting real feedback on unfinished work.

## How to apply these dials

Don't rewrite `FRAMEWORK.md`'s section skeleton per combination — instead, use the level/company/market dials to decide what to emphasize in each section, what constitutes a strong enough number for "outcome," and where the level bar sits when scoring against `AUDIT.md`. Use the tone dial only to re-voice how any of that gets said out loud — never to change what gets flagged or how it's scored.
